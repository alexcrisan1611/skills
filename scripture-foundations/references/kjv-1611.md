# The KJV as a 1611 Document: False Friends, Units, and Reversals

This is a reference file for the `scripture-foundations` skill. It covers the error that does the most damage when you reason from the King James Version: you read a modern English sense into a 1611 English word.

Every verse quoted below came from `scripture.ts`. Every count and every Strong's entry comes from the bundled tools. Run these commands from the skill folder to reproduce anything here:

```
bun run scripts/scripture.ts lookup "2 Thessalonians 2:7"
bun run scripts/lexicon.ts lookup G2722
```

If Bun is not installed, use `python scripts/scripture.py` and `python scripts/lexicon.py` with the same arguments.

## 1. Why this matters

The King James Version is not a modern translation. The translators produced it in 1611. The text that most people read today is not the 1611 printing. It is the 1769 Blayney edition, a revision of spelling, punctuation, and some wording. English changed between the two editions, and it changed again between 1769 and now.

This change produces a specific failure that repeats. A reader sees an English word that he knows. He gives it the meaning that it has today, and he reasons from that meaning. The reasoning can be sound and the conclusion can still be wrong, because the premise was never about the text.

Two properties make this problem worse than ordinary mistranslation:

- The words are common: `let`, `prevent`, `careful`, `want`, `suffer`, `quick`, `meat`, `talent`, `charity`. A reader gets no signal that something is wrong. An obscure word invites a footnote. A familiar word does not.
- The tools are cheap. `lexicon.ts english let` takes a second. It shows the whole family of Hebrew and Greek words that the KJV renders as "let", and there are 104 of them. A guess is no longer necessary. The purpose of this file is to make the check routine.

This file has a limit. A false friend tells you what a word does not mean. It rarely tells you what the word does mean. "Let" does not mean "permit" in 2 Thessalonians 2:7. That negative result is solid. What the verse positively asserts is a separate question, and you argue it from the Greek and the context. Keep the two questions apart.

A note on counts: the counts below come from `scripture.ts search`. The search folds case and matches substrings. A quoted count is the count of the tool for the given query string. It is not a lemma count (a count of one dictionary word in all its forms).

### Notes on individual entries

let. The KJV uses "let" for a very large number of different Hebrew and Greek words. `lexicon.ts english let` returns 104 entries. For that reason, you cannot assume one sense. On the Greek side, the sense "hinder" is clear. At 2 Thessalonians 2:7 the source is G2722 `katéchō`, _"to hold down (fast), in various applications (literally or figuratively)"_, glossed "have, hold (fast), keep (in memory), let, X make toward, possess, retain, seize on, stay, take, withhold". G2967 `kōlýō`, _"to estop, i.e. prevent (by word or act)"_, is glossed "forbid, hinder, keep from, let, not suffer, withstand". That entry puts the sense "hinder" and the gloss word "let" side by side. On the Hebrew side, most entries glossed "let" are words for release or for ceasing, not for restraint. An example is H2308 `chadal`, _"properly, to be flabby, i.e. (by implication) desist; (figuratively) be lacking or idle"_, glossed "cease, end, fall, forbear, forsake, leave (off), let alone, rest, be unoccupied, want". So the confusion is not a clean split between Hebrew and Greek. The English word "let" sits on top of two unrelated families of source words. To verify, run `lexicon.ts lookup G2722` and `lexicon.ts english let`.

prevent. The Hebrew behind Psalms 119:148 is H6923 `qadam`, _"to project (one self), i.e. precede; hence, to anticipate, hasten, meet (usually for help)"_, glossed "come (go, (flee)) before, disappoint, meet, prevent". On the Greek side are G5348 `phthánō` (_"to be beforehand, i.e. anticipate or precede"_) and G4399 `prophthánō`. All three words are about being ahead of something, not about stopping it. The query `search "prevent"` returns 17 verses in the KJV. In all of them, the older sense is the one that fits.

conversation. This is an older English noun from the verb "converse" in the sense "live, conduct oneself", not "talk". The query `search "conversation"` returns 20 verses. Psalms 37:14 "such as be of upright conversation" and Hebrews 13:5 "Let your conversation be without covetousness" make no sense as speech. Philippians 3:20 "our conversation is in heaven" is a weak example. The Greek there is G4175 `políteuma`, _"a community, i.e. (abstractly) citizenship (figuratively)"_. The verse speaks of citizenship, not of conduct. It does not mean that Christians talk about heaven, but it does not show the sense "conduct" well either. Use 1 Peter 3:1-2 or Philippians 1:27 for that sense.

careful. The word comes from "care" in the sense of anxiety: "full of care". Philippians 4:6 "Be careful for nothing" is not advice against diligence. It gives the same instruction as "take no thought" in Matthew 6. If you read it as "do not be attentive", you invert it.

quick. The word comes from Old English _cwic_, "alive". The modern sense "fast" is secondary and later. For this reason, "the quick and the dead" is not about speed. The older sense also survives in the phrase "cut to the quick" and in "quicksilver".

meat. Old English _mete_ meant food in general. Genesis 1:29 gives "every herb bearing seed" and "the fruit of a tree" "for meat". The KJV also renders many different Hebrew and Greek words as "meat". `lexicon.ts english meat` returns 35 entries. The reverse map is a substring search, so treat that number as an upper limit.

bowels. The Hebrew dictionary has H7356 `racham`, _"compassion (in the plural); by extension, the womb"_, glossed "bowels, compassion, damsel, tender love, (great, tender) mercy, pity, womb". The Greek is G4698 `splánchnon`, _"an intestine (plural); figuratively, pity or sympathy"_. Both dictionaries record the figurative sense ("pity") beside the literal one. In 1611 English the inner organs were the seat of feeling, so "bowels of mercies" was idiomatic. Today it only confuses, and for that reason modern versions say "compassion".

by and by. The phrase occurs in 4 verses. In 1611 it meant "immediately, at once". Mark 6:25 confirms it. The girl asks for the head "by and by", and the next verse but one, 6:27, opens "And immediately the king sent an executioner". At Luke 21:9 "the end is not by and by" means that the end is not immediate. That is the opposite of the modern "not soon".

peculiar. The word comes from Latin _peculium_, "private property". The Greek is G4041 `perioúsios`, _"being beyond usual, i.e. special (one's own)"_. The reverse map `english peculiar` returns G4041, G4047, and H5459. The "peculiar people" of the KJV are "a people that belongs to him", not "an odd people".

want. The word means "to lack". Psalms 23:1 "I shall not want" means "I shall not be in need", not "I shall not desire". Psalms 34:10 makes this explicit, because it pairs the word with hunger: "The young lions do lack, and suffer hunger: but they that seek the LORD shall not want any good thing." The query `search "want"` returns 54 verses.

ghost. The word means spirit or breath, from Old English _gāst_. The phrase "Holy Ghost" occurs in 89 verses and means exactly what "Holy Spirit" means. "Gave up the ghost" is a separate idiom for dying. The query `search "up the ghost"` returns 18 verses.

halt. The KJV uses this word for two different Hebrew words. At Genesis 32:31 the word is H6760 `tsala`, _"to limp (as if one-sided)"_. At 1 Kings 18:21 the word is H6452 `pasach`, _"to hop, i.e. (figuratively) skip over (or spare); by implication, to hesitate; also (literally) to limp, to dance"_. The reverse map `english halt` returns 10 entries. It is a substring match, so it also catches the unrelated word "shalt". This example shows why you must read the output of the reverse map and not only count it.

commend. The Greek is G3908 `paratíthēmi`, _"to place alongside, i.e. present (food, truth); by implication, to deposit (as a trust or for protection)"_, glossed "allege, commend, commit (the keeping of), put forth, set before". At Luke 23:46 "commend my spirit" is a deposit into safe keeping. The same verb gives "commit" elsewhere. The modern sense of praise is a poor fit.

charity. The reverse map `english charity` returns G26 alone. The query `search "charity"` returns 24 verses. The KJV uses "charity" for love, the sense of `agápē`, in 1 Corinthians 13. The sense "alms" also existed in English before 1611, but these verses do not use it. See section 4.

talent. The Greek is G5007 `tálanton`, _"a balance (as supporting weights), i.e. (by implication) a certain weight (and thence a coin or rather sum of money) or 'talent'"_. See section 4 and section 3.

estate. The word means state or condition. Verified examples include Psalms 136:23 "in our low estate" and Ecclesiastes 3:18 "the estate of the sons of men". The query `search "estate"` returns 17 verses. They include uses in the sense of condition (as above) and uses in the sense of property, such as Esther 1:19 "let the king give her royal estate unto another". The word is on this list because people now miss the sense of condition.

occupy. The word means "trade with". Luke 19:13 "Occupy till I come" is a business instruction. The parable gives out "ten pounds" and tells the servants to trade. Ezekiel 27:9 "to occupy thy merchandise" confirms the sense. The query returns only these 2 verses.

naughty. The word comes from "nought", meaning nothing. It means worthless, good for nothing. Jeremiah 24:2 "very naughty figs, which could not be eaten, they were so bad" makes the sense plain. The word occurs in only 3 verses.

corn. The word means any grain. Genesis 41:5 "ears of corn" and Matthew 12:1 "pluck the ears of corn" are wheat or barley, not maize. Maize is a crop of the Americas and did not grow in the ancient Near East. `english corn` returns 55 entries, but many of them match "corner", because the reverse map is a substring search.

coasts. The word means borders or territories. Matthew 2:16 "in all the coasts thereof" means the district around Bethlehem, not a beach. The "coasts of Judaea" in the KJV are regions, not shores.

usury. The word means any interest. The reverse map returns 6 entries. Among them are G5110 `tókos`, _"interest on money loaned (as a produce)"_, and H5383 `nashah`, _"to lend or (by reciprocity) borrow on security or interest"_. Exodus 22:25 prohibits "usury" between Israelites and says nothing about the rate. If you read "usury" as "predatory interest", you import a distinction that the 1611 word does not make.

meet. The word means suitable. Genesis 2:18 "an help meet for him" is "a helper suitable for him". "Meet" is an adjective. It is not a noun, and it is not part of a job title.

honest. The word means honorable, respectable, of good reputation. For this reason, the KJV can call things "honest" when they are matters of reputation and not of factual accuracy.

instant. The word means urgent and persistent. It comes from the same root as "insist". 2 Timothy 4:2 "be instant in season, out of season" means "keep at it, whether the moment suits or not". The modern sense (this exact moment) makes the second half of the clause incoherent.

replenish. See section 4. This word is in the table and section 4 expands it. It is a real false friend, although it is less well known than the big four.

firmament. The Hebrew is H7549 `raqia`, _"properly, an expanse, i.e. the firmament or (apparently) visible arch of the sky"_. The reverse map `english firmament` returns H7549 alone. The word "firmament" comes from Latin _firmamentum_, "a support or strengthening", through the Vulgate (the Latin Bible). So the solidity in the English word comes from the Latin chain of translation, not from the Hebrew.

substance. The word means property or possessions. Job 1:3 "His substance also was seven thousand sheep" lists wealth. Compare Hebrews 11:1, where the KJV renders G5287 `hypóstasis` as "substance" in a completely different sense. The same English word can sit on top of unrelated source words.

penny. See section 3. The KJV penny is a denarius.

## 3. The units and measures problem

The KJV keeps the source units. It says "shekel", "talent", "ephah", "bath", "cubit", "penny" (for the denarius), and "farthing". These are not English units, and no modern unit is an exact equivalent. This matters for reasoning in a specific way.

If you cannot convert a quantity, you cannot use it as a quantity. Suppose that you read "ten thousand talents" and think "a lot of money". Then you lose the number. But the number is often the point. Matthew 18 is a parable about a ratio: a debt that no one can pay is forgiven, and a trivial debt is pursued. The ratio exists only if the units exist. If you replace "talent" with "ability", as modern usage invites, the ratio disappears. Section 4 describes that failure.

### Relations the Bible states itself (verified)

These relations are safe, because the text gives the arithmetic:

| Relation                                   | Verified source |
| ------------------------------------------ | --------------- |
| 1 shekel = 20 gerahs                       | Exodus 30:13 "(a shekel is twenty gerahs:)", Leviticus 27:25 "twenty gerahs shall be the shekel" |
| 1 talent = 3,000 shekels                   | Derived. See the working below |
| 1 ephah = 1 bath = one-tenth of a homer    | Ezekiel 45:11 "that the bath may contain the tenth part of an homer, and the ephah the tenth part of an homer" |
| 1 omer = one-tenth of an ephah             | Exodus 16:36 "Now an omer is the tenth part of an ephah." |
| 1 denarius = the wage of a laborer for one day | Matthew 20:2 "when he had agreed with the labourers for a penny a day", Matthew 20:9 "they received every man a penny" |

The talent-to-shekel working. The Bible does not state "1 talent = 3,000 shekels" anywhere, but the numbers in Exodus 38 require it. Verified text:

- Exodus 38:25: "And the silver of them that were numbered of the congregation was an hundred talents, and a thousand seven hundred and threescore and fifteen shekels, after the shekel of the sanctuary:"
- Exodus 38:26: "A bekah for every man, that is, half a shekel, after the shekel of the sanctuary, for every one that went to be numbered, from twenty years old and upward, for six hundred thousand and three thousand and five hundred and fifty men."
- Numbers 1:46: "Even all they that were numbered were six hundred thousand and three thousand and five hundred and fifty."

The arithmetic:

```
603,550 men  x  0.5 shekel each        = 301,775 shekels total collected
301,775 shekels  -  1,775 shekels      = 300,000 shekels  (the "hundred talents")
300,000  /  100                        =   3,000 shekels per talent
```

This derivation is real, and you can check it against the text. Do it once, and "talent" stops being a vague word.

### Conversions that are estimates, not biblical data

These conversions need a weight standard that the Bible does not give. The bundled tools cannot verify them. Treat them as external historical estimates and cite your source:

| Unit                      | Approximate value                    | Notes |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ----- |
| cubit                     | about 18 in (45 cm)                  | The Strong's entry for H520 `ammah` describes it as the "fore-arm (below the elbow)". It is a unit based on the body, so it varies by definition. The query `search "cubit"` returns 141 verses. |
| shekel                    | about 0.4 oz (11-12 g) of silver     | A standard estimate from surviving weights. The text does not state it. |
| talent (weight)           | 3,000 shekels, about 73-79 lb (33-36 kg) of silver | Follows from the shekel estimate above: 3,000 x 11 g = 33 kg, and 3,000 x 12 g = 36 kg. |
| talent (value)            | about 6,000 denarii                  | Ancient metrology (the study of ancient weights and measures). It is not a biblical figure. See the arithmetic below. |
| the wage of a laborer     | about 1 denarius a day               | Biblical: Matthew 20:2, 20:9. |
| the working year of a laborer | about 300 days                   | An assumption, not biblical. The number of six-day weeks, festivals, and seasons is unknown. |

The talent-to-denarius arithmetic is conditional. If a talent was worth 6,000 denarii (an external estimate), then:

```
1 talent  = 6,000 denarii = 6,000 days' wages
6,000 days  /  300 working days per year  ≈  20 years of a laborer's wages

Matthew 18:24   10,000 talents  ≈ 60,000,000 denarii ≈ 200,000 years of wages
Matthew 18:28        100 pence   =        100 denarii  ≈   100 days of wages
Ratio            10,000 talents : 100 pence  =  600,000 : 1
```

The ratio is the point of the parable, and the ratio holds under error. Suppose that the talent-to-denarius figure is wrong by a factor of three. The debt that the master forgives is still five orders of magnitude larger than the debt that the servant pursues. Verified text: Matthew 18:24 "one was brought unto him, which owed him ten thousand talents." and Matthew 18:28 "found one of his fellowservants, which owed him an hundred pence".

Small coins. The penny is a denarius. The KJV uses "farthing" for two different Roman coins:

- G2835 `kodrántēs`, _"a quadrans, i.e. the fourth part of an as"_. KJV "farthing".
- G787 `assárion`, _"an assarius or as, a Roman coin"_. KJV "farthing".

The definitions show the problem. The `kodrantes` is the fourth part of an as, and the `assarion` is an as. So the KJV renders a coin and one quarter of that coin with the same English word. The two "farthings" are not the same coin.

The two sparrow verses use the same coin. In the standard Greek text, Matthew 10:29 and Luke 12:6 both use G787 `assárion`. Only Mark 12:42 uses G2835 `kodrántēs` ("two mites, which make a farthing"). The bundled tools do not tag verses with Strong's numbers, so this link comes from the standard concordance.

The prices compare directly:

- Matthew 10:29: "Are not two sparrows sold for a farthing?" That is 2 sparrows for 1 assarion.
- Luke 12:6: "Are not five sparrows sold for two farthings". That is 5 sparrows for 2 assaria.

At the rate in Matthew, 2 assaria buy 4 sparrows. Luke gives 5. Inference: a buyer who paid two farthings received a fifth sparrow free. Luke 12:6 then says of the five: "not one of them is forgotten before God". Not even the sparrow with no price is forgotten. Mark 12:42 shows how the small coins nest: "she threw in two mites, which make a farthing."

Revelation 6:6 tests whether you did the conversion: "A measure of wheat for a penny, and three measures of barley for a penny". This is a famine price. You cannot see that until you know that a penny is the wage for a full day.

## 4. Words whose modern sense reverses the argument

These are the dangerous words. Elsewhere, a false friend makes a sentence confusing. Here, it makes the sentence say the opposite of what it says.

### let: reverses 2 Thessalonians 2:7

Modern reading: "only he who now permits will permit". The sentence is odd, but not clearly broken, and a reader can pass over it.

1611 sense: to hinder. The Greek is G2722 `katéchō`, _"to hold down (fast)"_, glossed "hold (fast)", "withhold", "retain", "stay", "let". Verified text: 2 Thessalonians 2:7 "For the mystery of iniquity doth already work: only he who now letteth will let, until he be taken out of the way."

The reversal: the restrainer holds back the mystery of iniquity. He does not authorize it. If you read "let" as "permit", the verse says the opposite about who does what. It also says the opposite about whether something holds back the lawless one now. The verse becomes either trivial or inverted. The same word appears at Romans 1:13, where the sense is clear: "I purposed to come unto you, (but was let hitherto,)". Something hindered Paul from coming. Nothing permitted him to come.

Consequence: any argument from 2 Thessalonians 2 about restraint, lawlessness, or things held back stands or falls on this one word. The correct reading does not resolve the passage, because the identity of the restrainer is still disputed. But the direction of the sentence is settled.

### prevent: reverses 1 Thessalonians 4:15

Modern reading: "we shall not stop those who are asleep". This confuses readers, because the verse is plainly about resurrection.

1611 sense: to go before, to precede. H6923 `qadam` is _"to project (one self), i.e. precede"_, and G5348 `phthánō` is _"to be beforehand, i.e. anticipate or precede"_. Verified text: 1 Thessalonians 4:15 "we which are alive and remain unto the coming of the Lord shall not prevent them which are asleep." Also Psalms 119:148 "Mine eyes prevent the night watches, that I might meditate in thy word." The eyes of the psalmist get ahead of the night watches.

The reversal: with the modern sense, the verse seems to forbid the living to obstruct the dead. With the 1611 sense, it says that the living will not be raised before the dead. The order of resurrection is the whole content of the sentence, and the modern reading destroys it. The same word occurs at Job 30:27 "the days of affliction prevented me" and Psalms 88:13 "in the morning shall my prayer prevent thee".

Consequence: an argument about the order of the resurrection can invert its own conclusion here and still quote the verse accurately.

### charity: reverses 1 Corinthians 13:3

Modern reading: "charity" means relief given to the poor. Then "though I bestow all my goods to feed the poor... and have not charity" reads as a contradiction. Readers quietly resolve it with a second, spiritual kind of charity.

Sense in this chapter: love. The reverse map `english charity` returns G26 alone. G26 `agápē` has "love" first in its definition: _"love, i.e. affection or benevolence; specially (plural) a love-feast"_. The word "charity" occurs in 24 verses of the KJV, and the reverse map gives G26 as its only source word. The sense "alms" existed in English before 1611. The point is not that the word could not mean alms in 1611. The point is that the KJV uses "charity" for love (_agapē_) in 1 Corinthians 13.

The reversal: the verse does not say that gifts to the poor need an extra spiritual supplement. It says that the gift itself is worth nothing if it is not an act of love. The motive is part of the value of the act. It is not a bonus on top. The modern sense brings in a second virtue. The sense in the chapter makes it one virtue. Verified text: 1 Corinthians 13:3 "And though I bestow all my goods to feed the poor, and though I give my body to be burned, and have not charity, it profiteth me nothing."

Consequence: a common error in business reasoning from 1 Corinthians 13 reads it as "ethics is not enough, add spirituality". The text says something much sharper about what the act is. Look also at 1 Corinthians 13:13: "And now abideth faith, hope, charity, these three; but the greatest of these is charity." A modern reader hears "the greatest of these is philanthropy". The verse does not rank philanthropy.

### talent: reverses Matthew 25 entirely

Modern reading: "talent" means natural ability or aptitude. On this reading, the parable of the talents is about the use of your gifts.

Greek sense: a weight, and by extension a very large sum of money. G5007 `tálanton` is _"a balance (as supporting weights), i.e. (by implication) a certain weight (and thence a coin or rather sum of money) or 'talent'"_. The Hebrew equivalent is H3603 `kikkar`, _"a circle... also a (round) loaf; also a talent (or large [round] coin)"_. The KJV calls it "talent" in the Old Testament, for example Exodus 25:39 "Of a talent of pure gold shall he make it". Verified text: Matthew 25:15 "And unto one he gave five talents, to another two, and to another one; to every man according to his several ability". The query `search "talent"` returns 45 verses.

A note on the English word: the sense "natural ability" came from this parable. It was in English by the 15th century, so it is not a later sense than 1611. But the parable does not depend on the English word. The Greek word is a weight of money. Whatever the English word meant in 1611, the parable is about capital.

The reversal is total, and it produces a specific logical error. Look at what Matthew 25:15 actually says. The master distributes the money according to "his several ability", and then the servants trade with the money. Ability is the input that exists before the distribution, and the distribution rests on it. Ability is not the "talent". On the modern reading, "talents" are abilities, and the verse becomes circular: the master distributes abilities according to ability.

If you take the modern sense seriously, the arithmetic of the story breaks:

```
Sum entrusted        5 + 2 + 1  = 8 talents
Sum returned         10 + 4 + 1 = 15 talents
The unfaithful one   buried the money, returned it intact, and was condemned for it
```

None of this works with "abilities". You cannot bury an aptitude. You cannot double it by trade. You cannot return it unused. The modern reading also inverts the structure of accountability. On that reading, the master condemns the servant for not deploying a resource that was never his. The language of the passage is a master who settles accounts with servants over his own property. Verified text: Matthew 25:19 "After a long time the lord of those servants cometh, and reckoneth with them." and Matthew 25:21 "thou hast been faithful over a few things, I will make thee ruler over many things".

Consequence: every argument about "stewardship of your talents" that rests on ability quietly changes the subject of the text. The parable is about entrusted capital, accountability, and risk. Inference: that makes it a more direct business text than the ability reading. But you must cite it for what it says.

A related reversal: replenish. Genesis 1:28 "and replenish the earth" reads as "fill it again". That reading implies an earlier emptying and so a population before Adam. The Hebrew is H4390 `male`, _"to fill or (intransitively) be full of, in a wide application (literally and figuratively)"_. `english replenish` returns H4390 alone. The KJV also uses the English word "fill" in the same creation account, at Genesis 1:22 "fill the waters in the seas", and nobody reads a second filling there. In 1611, "replenish" meant "fill".

## 5. A verification checklist

Do these steps before you put a KJV word into an argument:

1. Find out if the word is on the list in section 2, or if it feels slightly wrong in context. If it does, stop. Do not reason from it until you complete step 2.
2. Find the source word. If the Strong's number is in question, use the reverse map: `lexicon.ts english <the KJV word>`. It gives candidate Strong's numbers. Read the output. It is a substring search, so "halt" also returns "shalt", and "corn" also returns "corner".
3. Read the definition, not only the gloss list. Run `lexicon.ts lookup <number>` and read the `def` line. The `KJV` gloss list tells you what the translators chose. The `def` line tells you what the word means. If the two disagree, the `def` line is the lexical claim.
4. Look at the whole gloss list for the other sense. If the gloss list does not contain the modern sense, the modern sense is probably wrong. If the list contains both senses, the word is ambiguous, and the context must decide. If the modern sense appears only as a minority entry, do not build on it. An example is "prudent" in "crafty, prudent, subtil" for H6175.
5. Verify the verse. Run `scripture.ts lookup "<book> <chapter>:<verse>"` and read the returned text. Quote it exactly. Do not cite a reference that you did not print. A reference that looks right but does not contain the word is the worst failure possible, and the tool takes one second.
6. Convert the units. If the argument uses a quantity (money, weight, distance, duration, or a ratio between quantities), convert it. If the conversion needs a figure that the text does not give, say so and name your source. Do not present an archaeological number as a biblical number.
7. State the direction of the argument. Write two things apart: (a) what the word means, and (b) what you conclude from it. If the conclusion reads as if the dictionary gave it, you merged two different kinds of claim. Put them in separate sentences, and label the inference.
