# The KJV as a 1611 Document: False Friends, Units, and Reversals

Reference file for the `scripture-foundations` skill. It covers the one error that
does the most damage when reasoning from the King James Version: reading a modern
English sense into a 1611 English word.

Every verse quoted below was pulled from `scripture.ts`; every count and every
Strong's entry comes from the bundled tools. Reproduce anything here with:

```
bun run skills/scripture-foundations/scripts/scripture.ts lookup "2 Thessalonians 2:7"
bun run skills/scripture-foundations/scripts/lexicon.ts lookup G2722
```

## 1. Why this matters

The King James Version is not a modern translation. It was produced in 1611, and the
text most people read today is not the 1611 printing: it is the **1769 Blayney edition**,
a revision in spelling, punctuation, and some wording. Between the two, and between
then and now, English changed.

That produces a specific and repeatable failure. A reader sees an English word they
know, assigns it the meaning it has today, and reasons from that. The reasoning can be
sound and the conclusion still be wrong, because the premise was never about the text
in the first place.

Two properties of the problem make it worse than ordinary mistranslation:

- **The words are common.** `let`, `prevent`, `careful`, `want`, `suffer`, `quick`,
  `meat`, `talent`, `charity`. A reader has no signal that anything is wrong. An
  obscure word invites a footnote; a familiar one does not.
- **The tools are cheap.** `lexicon.ts english "let"` takes a second and shows you the
  whole family of Hebrew and Greek words the KJV renders that way — 104 of them. There
  is no longer any excuse for guessing, and the entire point of this file is to make the
  check routine.

A word about what this file is not. Identifying a false friend tells you what a word
**does not** mean. It rarely tells you what a word **does** mean. "Let" does not mean
"permit" in 2 Thessalonians 2:7 — that is a negative result, and it is solid. What the
verse positively asserts is a separate question, argued from the Greek and the context.
Keep those two apart.

**A note on counts.** Counts below come from `scripture.ts search`, which folds case
and matches substrings. Where a count is quoted, it is the tool's count for the given
query string, not a lemma count.

### Notes on individual entries

**let.** The KJV uses "let" to render a very large number of different Hebrew and Greek
words: `lexicon.ts english "let"` returns **104 entries**. That is why no single sense can
be assumed. The hinder-sense on the Greek side is unambiguous. At 2 Thessalonians 2:7 the
source is G2722 `katéchō`, _"to hold down (fast), in various applications (literally or
figuratively)"_, glossed "have, hold (fast), keep (in memory), let, X make toward,
possess, retain, seize on, stay, take, withhold". And G2967 `kōlýō` — _"to estop, i.e.
prevent (by word or act)"_ — is glossed "forbid, hinder, keep from, let, not suffer,
withstand", which puts the hinder-sense and the gloss word "let" in the same entry. On
the Hebrew side the entries glossed "let" are mostly _release_ or _cease_ words rather
than restrain words — for example H2308 `chadal`, _"properly, to be flabby, i.e. (by
implication) desist; (figuratively) be lacking or idle"_, glossed "cease, end, fall,
forbear, forsake, leave (off), let alone, rest, be unoccupied, want". So the confusion is
not a neat Hebrew/Greek split: it is the English word "let" sitting on top of two
unrelated families of source words. Verify: `lexicon.ts lookup G2722` and
`lexicon.ts english "let"`.

**prevent.** The Hebrew behind Psalms 119:148 is H6923 `qadam` — _"to project (one
self), i.e. precede; hence, to anticipate, hasten, meet (usually for help)"_, glossed
"come (go, (flee)) before, disappoint, meet, prevent". The Greek side is G5348 `phthánō`
("_to be beforehand, i.e. anticipate or precede_") and G4399 `prophthánō`. Every one of
the three is about being **ahead of** something, not about stopping it. The query
`search "prevent"` returns 17 verses in the KJV, and in all of them the older sense is
the one that fits.

**conversation.** An older English noun from the verb "converse" in the sense of
"live, conduct oneself", not "talk". The query `search "conversation"` returns 20 verses.
Psalms 37:14 "such as be of upright conversation" and Hebrews 13:5 "Let your
conversation be without covetousness" make no sense as speech; Philippians 3:20 "our
conversation is in heaven" does not mean that Christians talk about heaven.

**careful.** From "care" in the sense of anxiety — "full of care". Philippians 4:6
"Be careful for nothing" is not advice against diligence; it is the same instruction as
"take no thought" in Matthew 6. Reading it as "don't be attentive" inverts it.

**quick.** From Old English _cwic_, alive. The modern sense of "fast" is secondary and
later. This is why "the quick and the dead" is not a racing reference. Note that the
same word survives in this older sense in the phrase "cut to the quick" and in "quicksilver".

**meat.** Old English _mete_ meant food generally. Genesis 1:29 gives "every herb
bearing seed" and "the fruit of a tree" "for meat". The KJV also renders many different
Hebrew and Greek words as "meat"; `lexicon.ts english "meat"` returns 35 entries, and
the reverse map is a substring search, so treat that number as an upper bound.

**bowels.** The Hebrew dictionary has H7356 `racham` — _"compassion (in the plural);
by extension, the womb"_ — glossed "bowels, compassion, damsel, tender love, (great,
tender) mercy, pity, womb". The Greek is G4698 `splánchnon` — _"an intestine (plural);
figuratively, pity or sympathy"_. Both dictionaries record that the figurative sense
("pity") sits alongside the literal one. In 1611 English the inner organs were the seat
of feeling, so "bowels of mercies" was idiomatic. It is now merely confusing, which is
why modern versions say "compassion".

**by and by.** The phrase occurs in 4 verses. In 1611 it meant "immediately, straight
away". Mark 6:25 confirms it: the girl asks for the head "by and by", and the very next
verse, 6:27, opens "And immediately the king sent an executioner". At Luke 21:9 "the end
is not by and by" means the end is not immediate — the opposite of the modern "not soon".

**peculiar.** From Latin _peculium_, private property. The Greek is G4041
`perioúsios` — _"being beyond usual, i.e. special (one's own)"_ — and the reverse map
`english "peculiar"` returns G4041, G4047, and H5459. The KJV's "peculiar people" means
"a people belonging to him", not "an odd people".

**want.** To lack. Psalms 23:1 "I shall not want" means "I shall not be in want", not
"I shall not desire". Psalms 34:10 makes it explicit by pairing it with hunger: "The
young lions do lack, and suffer hunger: but they that seek the LORD shall not want any
good thing." The query `search "want"` returns 54 verses.

**ghost.** Spirit, breath — from Old English _gāst_. The phrase "Holy Ghost" occurs in
89 verses and means exactly what "Holy Spirit" means. "Gave up the ghost" is a separate
idiom for dying; the query `search "up the ghost"` returns 18 verses.

**halt.** Two different Hebrew words are translated this way: H6760 `tsala` — _"to limp
(as if one-sided)"_ — at Genesis 32:31, and H6452 `pasach` — _"to hop, i.e.
(figuratively) skip over (or spare); by implication, to hesitate; also (literally) to
limp, to dance"_ — at 1 Kings 18:21. The reverse map `english "halt"` returns 10
entries, but it is a substring match, so it also catches the unrelated word "shalt".
This is a good illustration of why the reverse map's output must be read, not just counted.

**commend.** The Greek is G3908 `paratíthēmi` — _"to place alongside, i.e. present
(food, truth); by implication, to deposit (as a trust or for protection)"_ — glossed
"allege, commend, commit (the keeping of), put forth, set before". At Luke 23:46
"commend my spirit" is a deposit into safe keeping, and the same verb gives "commit"
elsewhere. The modern sense of commendation-as-praise is a poor fit.

**charity.** The reverse map `english "charity"` returns **G26 alone**, and the query
`search "charity"` returns 24 verses. In 1611, "charity" meant benevolent love —
precisely the sense of `agápē`. See §4.

**talent.** The Greek is G5007 `tálanton` — _"a balance (as supporting weights), i.e.
(by implication) a certain weight (and thence a coin or rather sum of money) or
'talent'"_. See §4 and §3.

**estate.** State or condition. Verified instances include Psalms 136:23 "in our low
estate" and Ecclesiastes 3:18 "the estate of the sons of men". The query
`search "estate"` returns 17 verses and includes both condition-sense uses (as above)
and property-sense uses such as Esther 1:19 "let the king give her royal estate unto
another". The word is on this list because the condition-sense is the one people now miss.

**occupy.** Trade with. Luke 19:13 "Occupy till I come" is a business instruction; the
parable hands out "ten pounds" and tells the servants to trade. Ezekiel 27:9 "to occupy
thy merchandise" confirms it. The query returns only these 2 verses.

**naughty.** From "nought", meaning nothing — worthless, good for nothing. Jeremiah 24:2
"very naughty figs, which could not be eaten, they were so bad" makes the sense plain.
Only 3 verses.

**corn.** Any grain. Genesis 41:5 "ears of corn" and Matthew 12:1 "pluck the ears of
corn" are wheat or barley, not maize — maize is a New World crop and did not exist in
the ancient Near East. Note that `english "corn"` returns 55 entries but many are
matches on "corner", because the reverse map is a substring search.

**coasts.** Borders or territories. Matthew 2:16 "in all the coasts thereof" means
throughout the surrounding district of Bethlehem, not along a beach. The KJV's "coasts
of Judaea" is regional, not maritime.

**usury.** Any interest. The reverse map returns 6 entries; among them G5110 `tókos`
— _"interest on money loaned (as a produce)"_ — and H5383 `nashah` — _"to lend or (by
reciprocity) borrow on security or interest"_. Exodus 22:25 prohibits "usury" between
Israelites without reference to rate. Reading "usury" as "predatory interest" imports a
distinction the 1611 word does not make.

**meet.** Suitable. Genesis 2:18 "an help meet for him" is "a helper suitable for him";
"meet" is an adjective, not a noun and not part of a job title.

**honest.** Honourable, respectable, of good reputation. This is why the KJV can speak
of "honest" things that are matters of reputation rather than factual accuracy.

**instant.** Urgent and persistent, from the same root as "insist". 2 Timothy 4:2
"be instant in season, out of season" means to keep at it whether or not the moment
suits. The modern sense (this precise moment) makes the second half of the clause
incoherent.

**replenish.** See §4 — this one is included in the table and expanded there because
it is a genuine false friend even though it is less well known than the big four.

**firmament.** The Hebrew is H7549 `raqia` — _"properly, an expanse, i.e. the
firmament or (apparently) visible arch of the sky"_. The reverse map
`english "firmament"` returns H7549 alone. The word "firmament" comes from Latin
_firmamentum_, "a support or strengthening", via the Vulgate. So the solidity implied
by the English word is an artefact of the Latin translation chain, not of the Hebrew.

**substance.** Property or possessions. Job 1:3 "His substance also was seven thousand
sheep" itemizes wealth. Contrast Hebrews 11:1, where the KJV renders G5287 `hypóstasis`
as "substance" in a completely different sense — a reminder that the same English word
can sit on top of unrelated source words.

**penny.** See §3. A denarius.

## 3. The units and measures problem

The KJV preserves the source units. It says "shekel", "talent", "ephah", "bath",
"cubit", "denarius", "farthing". Those are not English units, and no modern unit is an
exact equivalent. That matters for reasoning in a specific way:

**A quantity you cannot convert is not available to you as a quantity.** If you read
"ten thousand talents" and translate it mentally to "a lot of money", you have thrown
away the number. But the number is often the point. Matthew 18 is a parable about
**ratio**: an unpayable debt forgiven, and a trivial debt pursued. The ratio only
exists if the units exist. And the moment you replace "talent" with "ability", as
modern usage invites, the ratio disappears entirely — which is exactly the failure in §4.

### Relations the Bible states itself (verified)

These are the safe ones, because the text supplies the arithmetic:

| Relation                                   | Verified source                                                                                                        |
| ------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1 shekel = 20 gerahs                       | Exodus 30:13 "(a shekel is twenty gerahs:)"; Leviticus 27:25 "twenty gerahs shall be the shekel"                       |
| 1 talent = 3,000 shekels                   | Derived — see the working below                                                                                        |
| 1 ephah = 1 bath = one-tenth of a homer    | Ezekiel 45:11 "that the bath may contain the tenth part of an homer, and the ephah the tenth part of an homer"         |
| 1 omer = one-tenth of an ephah             | Exodus 16:36 "Now an omer is the tenth part of an ephah."                                                              |
| 1 denarius = one day's wage for a labourer | Matthew 20:2 "when he had agreed with the labourers for a penny a day"; Matthew 20:9 "they received every man a penny" |

**The talent-to-shekel working.** The Bible does not state "1 talent = 3,000 shekels"
anywhere, but the numbers in Exodus 38 force it. Verified text:

- Exodus 38:25 — "And the silver of them that were numbered of the congregation was an
  hundred talents, and a thousand seven hundred and threescore and fifteen shekels, after
  the shekel of the sanctuary:"
- Exodus 38:26 — "A bekah for every man, that is, half a shekel, after the shekel of the
  sanctuary, for every one that went to be numbered, from twenty years old and upward,
  for six hundred thousand and three thousand and five hundred and fifty men."
- Numbers 1:46 — "Even all they that were numbered were six hundred thousand and three
  thousand and five hundred and fifty."

The arithmetic:

```
603,550 men  x  0.5 shekel each        = 301,775 shekels total collected
301,775 shekels  -  1,775 shekels      = 300,000 shekels  (the "hundred talents")
300,000  /  100                        =   3,000 shekels per talent
```

This is a real, checkable derivation from the text. It is worth doing once so that
"talent" stops being a vague word.

### Conversions that are estimates, not biblical data

These require a weight standard the Bible does not specify. **The bundled tools cannot
verify these.** Treat them as external historical estimates and cite your source:

| Unit                      | Approximate value                    | Notes                                                                                                                                                                                       |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| cubit                     | ≈ 45 cm / 18 in                      | The Strong's entry for H520 `ammah` describes it as the "fore-arm (below the elbow)" — an anthropometric unit, hence variable by definition. The query `search "cubit"` returns 141 verses. |
| shekel                    | ≈ 11–12 g of silver                  | Standard estimate from surviving weights; not stated in the text.                                                                                                                           |
| talent (weight)           | ≈ 3,000 shekels ≈ 30–34 kg of silver | Follows from the shekel estimate above.                                                                                                                                                     |
| talent (value)            | ≈ 6,000 denarii                      | Ancient metrology; **not a biblical figure.** See the arithmetic below.                                                                                                                     |
| a labourer's wage         | ≈ 1 denarius per day                 | Biblical: Matthew 20:2, 20:9.                                                                                                                                                               |
| a labourer's working year | ≈ 300 days                           | An assumption, not biblical. Six-day weeks, festivals, and seasons are all unknown.                                                                                                         |

**The talent-to-denarius arithmetic, clearly labelled as conditional.** If a talent was
worth 6,000 denarii (external estimate), then:

```
1 talent  = 6,000 denarii = 6,000 days' wages
6,000 days  /  300 working days per year  ≈  20 years of a labourer's wages

Matthew 18:24   10,000 talents  ≈ 60,000,000 denarii ≈ 200,000 years of wages
Matthew 18:28        100 pence   =        100 denarii  ≈   100 days of wages
Ratio            10,000 talents : 100 pence  =  600,000 : 1
```

The **ratio** is the point of the parable, and the ratio is robust: even if the
talent-to-denarius figure is off by a factor of three, the debt forgiven still dwarfs
the debt pursued by five orders of magnitude. Verified text: Matthew 18:24 "one was
brought unto him, which owed him ten thousand talents." and Matthew 18:28 "found one of
his fellowservants, which owed him an hundred pence".

**Small coins.** The penny is a denarius. The "farthing" is two different Roman coins,
whichever the source word was:

- G2835 `kodrántēs` — _"a quadrans, i.e. the fourth part of an as"_ — KJV "farthing"
- G787 `assárion` — _"an assarius or as, a Roman coin"_ — KJV "farthing"

Note the confusion: `kodrantes` is said in its own definition to be the _fourth part of
an as_, while `assarion` is _an as_. So the KJV renders both a coin and one quarter of
that coin with the same English word, and the two "farthings" are not the same coin.
The sparrow prices are consistent once you convert. Matthew 10:29 "Are not two sparrows
sold for a farthing?" gives 2 sparrows per quadrans. Luke 12:6 "Are not five sparrows
sold for two farthings" gives 5 sparrows per 2 asses — and since 1 as is 4 quadrantes,
that is 5 sparrows per 8 quadrantes, or 1.6 sparrows per quadrans. Same order of
magnitude, in two different Gospels, in two different currencies. Mark 12:42 confirms
the nesting of the small coins: "she threw in two mites, which make a farthing."

Revelation 6:6 is a good test of whether you have done the conversion: "A measure of
wheat for a penny, and three measures of barley for a penny" is a famine price, and you
cannot see that until you know a penny is a full day's wage.

## 4. Words whose modern sense reverses the argument

These are the dangerous ones. Elsewhere a false friend makes a sentence confusing.
Here it makes the sentence say the opposite of what it says.

### let — reverses 2 Thessalonians 2:7

**Modern reading:** "only he who now permits will permit" — an odd but not obviously
broken sentence, and easy to read past.

**1611 sense:** to hinder. The Greek is G2722 `katéchō`, _"to hold down (fast)"_,
glossed "hold (fast)", "withhold", "retain", "stay", "let". Verified text: 2
Thessalonians 2:7 "For the mystery of iniquity doth already work: only he who now
letteth will let, until he be taken out of the way."

**The reversal:** the restrainer is the one _holding back_ the mystery of iniquity, not
the one _authorising_ it. Read as "permit", the verse says the opposite about who is
doing what and about whether the lawless one is currently being held back. The verse
becomes either trivial or inverted. The same word appears at Romans 1:13, where the sense
is unambiguous: "I purposed to come unto you, (but was let hitherto,)" — Paul was
_hindered_ from coming, not _permitted_ to come.

**Consequence:** any argument from 2 Thessalonians 2 about restraint, lawlessness, or
things being held back stands or falls on this one word. Note that reading it correctly
does not resolve the passage — the identity of the restrainer remains disputed. But the
direction of the sentence is settled.

### prevent — reverses 1 Thessalonians 4:15

**Modern reading:** "we shall not stop those who are asleep" — which trips readers up,
since the verse is plainly about resurrection.

**1611 sense:** to go before, to precede. H6923 `qadam`, _"to project (one self), i.e.
precede"_; G5348 `phthánō`, _"to be beforehand, i.e. anticipate or precede"_. Verified
text: 1 Thessalonians 4:15 "we which are alive and remain unto the coming of the Lord
shall not prevent them which are asleep." And Psalms 119:148 "Mine eyes prevent the
night watches, that I might meditate in thy word." — the psalmist's eyes _get ahead of_
the night watches.

**The reversal:** with the modern sense, the verse appears to forbid the living from
obstructing the dead. With the 1611 sense, it says the living will not be raised
_before_ the dead. The order of resurrection is the whole content of the sentence, and
it is destroyed by the modern reading. Same word at Job 30:27 "the days of affliction
prevented me" and Psalms 88:13 "in the morning shall my prayer prevent thee".

**Consequence:** eschatological arguments about resurrection order can invert their own
conclusion here while quoting the verse accurately.

### charity — reverses 1 Corinthians 13:3

**Modern reading:** "charity" = relief given to the poor. So "though I bestow all my
goods to feed the poor... and have not charity" reads as a contradiction, and readers
quietly resolve it by assuming a second, spiritual kind of charity.

**1611 sense:** benevolent love. The reverse map `english "charity"` returns **G26
alone**, and G26 `agápē` is glossed "love" first: _"love, i.e. affection or benevolence;
specially (plural) a love-feast"_. All 24 occurrences of "charity" in the KJV are this
one Greek word.

**The reversal:** the verse is not saying that giving to the poor requires some extra
spiritual supplement. It is saying that the _giving itself_ is worth nothing if it is
not an act of love — that the motive and disposition are constitutive of the value of
the act, not a bonus on top. The modern sense smuggles in a second virtue; the 1611
sense collapses it into one. Verified text: 1 Corinthians 13:3 "And though I bestow all
my goods to feed the poor, and though I give my body to be burned, and have not charity,
it profiteth me nothing."

**Consequence:** this is the single most common error in business reasoning from
1 Corinthians 13 — reading it as "ethics is not enough, add spirituality". The text says
something much sharper about the identity of the act. Note also 1 Corinthians 13:13:
"And now abideth faith, hope, charity, these three; but the greatest of these is
charity." A modern reader hears "the greatest of these is philanthropy", which is not
what is being ranked.

### talent — reverses Matthew 25 entirely

**Modern reading:** "talent" = natural ability or aptitude. On this reading the parable
of the talents is about using your gifts.

**1611 sense:** a weight, and by extension a very large sum of money. G5007 `tálanton`
— _"a balance (as supporting weights), i.e. (by implication) a certain weight (and
thence a coin or rather sum of money) or 'talent'"_. The Hebrew equivalent H3603 `kikkar`
— _"a circle... also a (round) loaf; also a talent (or large [round] coin)"_ — is what
the KJV calls "talent" in the Old Testament, e.g. Exodus 25:39 "Of a talent of pure gold
shall he make it". Verified text: Matthew 25:15 "And unto one he gave five talents, to
another two, and to another one; to every man according to his several ability". The
query `search "talent"` returns 45 verses.

**The reversal is total, and it produces a specific logical error.** Note what
Matthew 25:15 actually says: the money is distributed according to "his several
ability", and _then_ the servants trade with the money. Ability is the _pre-existing
input_ on which distribution is based. It is not the "talent". On the modern reading,
"talents" are abilities, and the verse becomes circular: the master distributes
abilities according to ability.

Take the modern sense seriously and the story's arithmetic is destroyed:

```
Sum entrusted        5 + 2 + 1  = 8 talents
Sum returned         10 + 4 + 1 = 15 talents
The unfaithful one   buried the money, returned it intact, and was condemned for it
```

None of that can be said about "abilities". You cannot bury an aptitude, you cannot
double it by trading, and you cannot return it unused. And the modern reading inverts
the accountability structure: the condemned servant is condemned for _not deploying a
resource that was never his_, while the passage's own language is a master settling
accounts with servants over his own property. Verified text: Matthew 25:19 "After a long
time the lord of those servants cometh, and reckoneth with them." and Matthew 25:21
"thou hast been faithful over a few things, I will make thee ruler over many things".

**Consequence:** every "stewardship of your talents" argument built on ability has
quietly exchanged the text's subject matter. The parable is about entrusted capital,
accountability, and risk — which is if anything a more direct business text than the
ability reading, but you have to cite it for what it says.

**Bonus reversal, same family: replenish.** Genesis 1:28 "and replenish the earth"
reads as "fill it again", implying a prior emptying and hence a pre-Adamic population.
The Hebrew is H4390 `male` — _"to fill or (intransitively) be full of, in a wide
application (literally and figuratively)"_ — and `english "replenish"` returns H4390
alone. The KJV also uses the English word "fill" in the same creation account, at
Genesis 1:22 "fill the waters in the seas", where nobody reads a re-filling. "Replenish"
in 1611 simply meant "fill".

## 5. A verification checklist

Run this before putting any KJV word into an argument.

1. **Is the word on the §2 list, or does it feel slightly off in context?** If yes, stop.
   Do not reason from it until step 2 is done.
2. **Find the source word.** If the Strong's number is in question, use the reverse map:
   `lexicon.ts english "<the KJV word>"`. That gives you candidate Strong's numbers.
   **Read the output** — it is a substring search, so "halt" also returns "shalt" and
   "corn" also returns "corner".
3. **Read the definition, not just the gloss list.** Run
   `lexicon.ts lookup <number>` and read the `def` line. The `KJV` gloss list tells you
   what the translators chose; the `def` line tells you what the word means. When the two
   pull apart, the `def` line is the lexical claim.
4. **Check the whole gloss list for the other sense.** If the modern sense is absent from
   the gloss list, the modern sense is probably wrong. If both senses are present, the
   word is genuinely ambiguous and context must decide. If the modern sense appears but
   in a minority position (as "prudent" does among "crafty, prudent, subtil" for H6175),
   do not build on it.
5. **Verify the verse.** Run `scripture.ts lookup "<book> <chapter>:<verse>"` and read
   the returned text. Quote it exactly. **Do not cite a reference you have not printed** —
   a plausible-looking reference that does not contain the word is the most embarrassing
   possible failure, and the tool costs one second.
6. **Check the units.** If the argument involves any quantity — money, weight, distance,
   duration, or a ratio between quantities — convert it. If the conversion needs a figure
   the text does not supply, say so and name your source. Do not let a number pass as
   biblical when it is archaeological.
7. **State the direction of the argument.** Write down separately (a) what the word means
   and (b) what you conclude from it. If the conclusion reads as though the dictionary
   supplied it, you have merged two different kinds of claim. Keep them in separate
   sentences, or at least in separate clauses, and label the inference.
