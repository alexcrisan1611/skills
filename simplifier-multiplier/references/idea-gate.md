# The idea gate

A multiplier copies after its kind (A11), so decide what is worth multiplying before you build the multiplier. The gate tests an idea against six criteria. Every one is required: an idea that fails one is a no, however good it looks otherwise.

The gate is also a guard for the person who brings the idea. People often assume their idea is fine. Your job at the gate is to push back at the first sign of a mismatch and to ask questions until the idea either fits or is dropped.

## The six criteria

| # | Criterion | The question | A sign of a mismatch |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | First-hand need | Do you, or the people you know and serve, have this problem now? | "People will want this"; a need known only from research or trends |
| 2 | Purpose beyond the market | What good does it serve besides revenue? | The only answer is money, reach or status |
| 3 | Existing tools fail | What do people use today, and where exactly does it fail them? | "There's nothing like it" with no search done; the failure is a matter of taste |
| 4 | Seen whole | Can you describe the finished product in a few plain sentences? | A list of features with no centre; the description changes each time it is asked |
| 5 | Lasting | Will people come back to it, and remember it exists? | A one-off use; a tool you forget about after the first try |
| 6 | Not a feature in waiting | Could a platform (the browser, the phone's system, a large app) ship this as a built-in feature? | It sits on top of one platform and does one small thing that platform could add |

Criterion 4 is the Habakkuk 2:2 test applied to the idea itself: if it cannot be made "plain upon tables," it is not simple yet, and a multiplier cannot copy it.

## Who checks each criterion

The gate follows the threshold of Exodus 18:22. Research is a small matter, and the agent does it. The judgment that only the owner of the idea can make is a great matter, and it goes to them.

| Criterion | Who checks it |
|---|---|
| 3. Existing tools fail | The agent researches the alternatives, their reviews and their prices |
| 6. Not a feature in waiting | The agent checks platform features and announced roadmaps |
| 2. Purpose beyond the market | The agent drafts the case; the owner confirms it |
| 5. Lasting | The agent looks for signs of repeat use in similar products; the owner confirms |
| 1. First-hand need | The owner rules |
| 4. Seen whole | The owner rules |

## How to push back

1. Push back at the first resemblance of a mismatch, not only at a clear failure. A vague answer, a criterion that is assumed but not shown, enthusiasm offered in place of evidence, "everyone needs this," or a large platform working near the same space are all reasons to stop and ask.
2. Name the criterion and the sign you saw, in one sentence. Do not hide the concern inside a compliment.
3. Ask one question at a time, and hear the whole answer before you judge. "He that answereth a matter before he heareth it, it is folly and shame unto him" (Proverbs 18:13). "Counsel in the heart of man is like deep water; but a man of understanding will draw it out" (Proverbs 20:5).
4. Ask questions that let the person find the answer, not questions that argue. The aim is alignment, not winning.
5. Keep going until the criterion is shown or plainly fails. If the answers do not settle it, say so and mark it open.
6. Never rule on criteria 1 and 4 yourself. Report what you heard and let the owner decide.

Pushing back is a kindness, not an obstacle. "Faithful are the wounds of a friend; but the kisses of an enemy are deceitful" (Proverbs 27:6). "Iron sharpeneth iron; so a man sharpeneth the countenance of his friend" (Proverbs 27:17). Agreeing with an idea that does not fit, to keep the conversation pleasant, is the deceitful kiss.

Counting the cost comes before building: "which of you, intending to build a tower, sitteth not down first, and counteth the cost, whether he have sufficient to finish it?" (Luke 14:28). The gate is that sitting down.

## The verdict

Give one of three verdicts, with the reason for each criterion:

- Pass: all six are shown. The idea goes on to the rest of the procedure.
- Refine: one or more criteria are open, not failed. Name exactly what would close each one.
- No: one or more criteria plainly fail. Say which, and why. A no judges the idea, never the person.

Invite the owner to commit the decision to God: "Commit thy works unto the LORD, and thy thoughts shall be established" (Proverbs 16:3).

Keep the gate short for a passing remark. Run it in full when someone is about to commit time or money to building.

## Where the criteria came from

The criteria were drawn from one clear yes and one weak yes in a real portfolio:

- The clear yes was a prayer and worship product. The need was first-hand, it served the church, the existing tools failed, and the owner could see the finished product from the start. That gave criteria 1 to 4.
- The weak yes was a small browser helper. People forgot it existed, and the browser was likely to ship the same thing as a built-in feature. That gave criteria 5 and 6.

When you adapt the gate for another person, draw their criteria the same way: compare a clear yes with a clear no from their own history before you add or remove anything.
