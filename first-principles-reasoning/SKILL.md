# First Principles Reasoning

## What this skill is for

Most reasoning is pattern matching. Someone did something that worked, somewhere, and the
result became advice. Pattern matching is cheap and usually correct. It fails when the ground
under the pattern has moved.

This skill is the general engine for reasoning from the ground up. It has one job: establish
what is actually given before anything is concluded from it.

It is not an argument for doubt. A person who questions everything knows nothing, because
every argument needs a floor. The work here is finding the floor, testing it, then building
on it without re-testing it every hour.

Two rules govern the whole skill.

**Applications are inferences.** When this skill reasons from Scripture, the result is an
inference drawn by a fallible reader. Scripture is the source. The application is the work.

**The method has a limit.** Some questions cannot be settled from inside the system. This
skill names those limits rather than covering them. The full treatment is in
`references/epistemic-limits.md`.

---

## What a first principle is

A first principle is a claim that cannot be derived from anything more basic, and on which
the rest of the argument rests.

The test is simple. If you can derive it from something else, it is not first. It is a
conclusion wearing the clothes of a starting point.

Three categories get confused here.

| Category          | What it is                     | How to treat it                |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------ |
| First principle   | Cannot be reduced further      | Name it, state it, build on it |
| Derived principle | Follows from a first principle | Show the derivation            |
| Assumption        | Inherited and never examined   | Test it, then reclassify it    |

The labels **Text states**, **Lexical**, **Inference**, and **Contested** mark the status of a
claim throughout this skill. An axiom quoted from `business-first-principles` keeps its number
(A1 to A34) and its label.

## The regress and where it stops

An argument cannot go back forever. Each justification needs another one, and an endless chain
justifies nothing. Reasoning terminates at what cannot be reduced.

Scripture opens at that point. Genesis 1:1 states, "In the beginning God created the heaven
and the earth." **(Text states)** The book begins with an act rather than an argument. Nothing
justifies the act, because nothing precedes it.

- **Lexical:** the KJV word "beginning" in Genesis 1:1 is rendered from a Hebrew family that
  includes H7225 _reshiyth_, defined as "the first, in place, time, order or rank
  (specifically, a firstfruit)". The word carries priority of order, and not only priority of
  time.

Aristotle used the word _archai_ for starting points that cannot be demonstrated.
**(Contested, in the narrow sense)** His accounts differ across his works, and the term itself
is standard. The Greek word is G746 _archē_, defined as "a commencement, or (concretely) chief
(in various applications of order, time, place, or rank)".

Hebrews 5:12 is the one verse in the KJV that contains the phrase "first principles". A text
search returns exactly one verse. It reads, "For when for the time ye ought to be teachers, ye
have need that one teach you again which be the first principles of the oracles of God; and
are become such as have need of milk, and not of strong meat."

- **Lexical:** the word rendered "principles" there is G4747 _stoicheion_, defined as
  "something orderly in arrangement, i.e. (by implication) a serial (basal, fundamental,
  initial) constituent (literally), proposition (figuratively)". The gloss list for the entry
  is "element, principle, rudiment".
- **Text states:** Hebrews 5:12 to 6:2 continues with the list. Verses 6:1 and 6:2 name the
  items: "repentance from dead works, and of faith toward God, Of the doctrine of baptisms, and
  of laying on of hands, and of resurrection of the dead, and of eternal judgment." That is
  six items.
- **Contested:** the passage describes doctrine, not method. Reading it as a method for
  reasoning is an inference, and the text does not make that move.

Verse 6:1 uses a different word for "principles". It is G746 _archē_, the same word Aristotle
used, and the first entry in the KJV gloss list for G746 is "beginning". **(Lexical)** Newer
translations call the passage "the elementary doctrine" or "the foundation".

## Orientation before technique

Method is not the first requirement. The starting condition of the thinker is.

Proverbs 1:7 states, "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge: but fools despise
wisdom and instruction." Proverbs 9:10 states, "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of
wisdom: and the knowledge of the holy is understanding." **(Text states)**

Note what the claim is about. Both verses attach the beginning to a disposition rather than to
a procedure. **(Inference)** A method applied without that orientation produces knowledge, and
the knowledge is real. It is built on the wrong floor.

- **Lexical:** "instruction" in Proverbs 1:7 is among the KJV renderings of H4148 _musar_,
  defined as "properly, chastisement; figuratively, reproof, warning or instruction; also
  restraint". The word carries correction as well as teaching. A learner who cannot be
  corrected has not entered the category.

Proverbs 8:22 to 8:31 places wisdom at the creation. Wisdom speaks: "The LORD possessed me in
the beginning of his way, before his works of old" (8:22). The passage ends, "Then I was by
him, as one brought up with him: and I was daily his delight, rejoicing always before him;
Rejoicing in the habitable part of his earth; and my delights were with the sons of men"
(8:30-31).

The consequence for reasoning is large. **(Inference)** If the order of the world is prior to
the human mind and independent of it, then fitting explanation is possible. The world is
intelligible because a mind made it, and it is stable because that mind is constant. On this
account, reasoning is discovery rather than invention.

- **Contested:** the KJV says wisdom was "possessed" (8:22). The Hebrew H7069 _qanah_ is
  defined as "to erect, i.e. create; by extension, to procure, especially by purchase
  (causatively, sell); by implication to own". Readers have long disputed whether the sense in
  this verse is possession or begetting. The dispute does not touch the point above, which
  rests on the whole passage rather than on one verb.

## The limit of the instrument

A method that cannot state its own limit will overstate its results.

Isaiah 55:8-9 states the distance: "For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your
ways my ways, saith the LORD. For as the heavens are higher than the earth, so are my ways
higher than your ways, and my thoughts than your thoughts." **(Text states)**

Romans 11:33-36 closes a long argument with the same note: "O the depth of the riches both of
the wisdom and knowledge of God! how unsearchable are his judgments, and his ways past finding
out! For who hath known the mind of the Lord? or who hath been his counsellor?" **(Text
states)**

1 Corinthians 13:9-12 states the condition of the knower: "For we know in part, and we
prophesy in part... For now we see through a glass, darkly; but then face to face: now I know
in part; but then shall I know even as also I am known."

- **Lexical:** "glass" is G2072 _esoptron_, "a mirror (for looking into)". "Darkly" is G135
  _ainigma_, "an obscure saying ('enigma'), i.e. (abstractly) obscureness". The image is a
  mirror in poor light, and not total darkness.
- **Text states:** the limit is temporary. Verse 13:12 ends with knowledge completed. Partial
  knowledge is the current condition rather than the permanent one.

Deuteronomy 29:29 draws the boundary in one sentence: "The secret things belong unto the LORD
our God: but those things which are revealed belong unto us and to our children for ever, that
we may do all the words of this law." **(Text states)** The verse pairs a limit with a grant.
The limit is real, and it is not total. What is revealed is given for action, and the purpose
clause at the end says so.

- **Lexical:** "secret" is among the renderings of H5641 _sathar_, "to hide (by covering),
  literally or figuratively".

Job 38 to 41 supplies the longest treatment. God answers Job out of a whirlwind with questions
rather than explanations. Job 38:4 reads, "Where wast thou when I laid the foundations of the
earth? declare, if thou hast understanding." Job 38:33 reads, "Knowest thou the ordinances of
heaven? canst thou set the dominion thereof in the earth?" Job 42:3 reads, "therefore have I
uttered that I understood not; things too wonderful for me, which I knew not." Job 42:5-6
reads, "I have heard of thee by the hearing of the ear: but now mine eye seeth thee. Wherefore
I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes." **(Text states)**

The book never tells Job why he suffered. It establishes who is speaking. **(Text states)**
The response to the limit is not an answer. It is a corrected posture. **(Inference)**

A note on method follows from these texts. When a question has no available answer, a
confident story about the answer is worse than the admission, because the story gets used.
**(Inference)**

## The failure modes

Six failure modes do most of the damage. Each one has a test.

**1. The inherited assumption.** A claim arrived without a decision. It came from a family, a
school, a profession, or a feed. It feels like a fact because nobody has examined it.
The test: name the source. If the answer is "everyone knows", the claim is in this category.
Proverbs 18:17 states the correction: "He that is first in his own cause seemeth just; but his
neighbour cometh and searcheth him." **(Text states)** The first account of anything sounds
just. Only the opposing account tests it.

**2. The substituted authority.** The question "is it true?" gets answered with "who says so?".
The authority can be a person, a credential, a market, a majority, or a study. Authority is
evidence, but it is not the claim itself.
The test: state the claim as one sentence, then ask what supports the sentence apart from the
reputation of the speaker. Acts 17:11 states the practice: the Bereans "received the word with
all readiness of mind, and searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so."
**(Text states)**

**3. The worship of an analogy.** A model that fitted one situation is applied to another. The
map becomes the territory. Financial models, biological metaphors, military language, and
software patterns all produce this error.
The test: name the point where the analogy breaks. An analogy with no stated limit is being
used as a proof, and it cannot carry that weight.

**4. The false dichotomy.** Two options are offered, and a third is refused. "We cut the budget
or we miss the date." Both branches are usually real. The frame itself is the error.
The test: ask what the two options share. A shared premise is usually the thing to attack.

**5. The undisclosed premise.** A step in the argument is skipped. The conclusion rests on a
claim that was never stated and never defended.
The test: write the argument as numbered lines, one claim per line. A gap becomes visible on
paper and invisible in speech.

**6. Haste.** The conclusion arrives before the evidence. Haste is not only a vice. It is a
predictable output of pressure, and it produces work that fails every test above.
Isaiah 28:16 supplies the counter: "Behold, I lay in Zion for a foundation a stone, a tried
stone, a precious corner stone, a sure foundation: he that believeth shall not make haste."
**(Text states)**

- **Lexical:** "make haste" is among the renderings of H2363 _chush_, defined as "to hurry;
  figuratively, to be eager with excitement or enjoyment".
- **Text states:** the verse ties confidence in the foundation to unhurried action. Belief and
  pace appear in the same sentence.

Luke 14:28-30 states the cost of the opposite. The builder who starts without counting "is not
able to finish it", and onlookers mock the half-built tower. **(Text states)**

## The derivation ladder

Work climbs in four rungs. The word "ladder" matters, because each rung rests on the one below
it.

1. **Observation.** What happened? Record it with a measure and a date. "Support tickets rose
   40 percent last quarter." This is the only rung that is not an interpretation.
2. **Pattern.** What keeps happening? A pattern needs repetition across time or across cases.
   "Ticket volume rises whenever a release ships without a help article."
3. **Principle.** What rule explains the pattern? A principle covers the observed cases and
   predicts new ones. "Users contact support when a change is not explained at the point of
   use."
4. **Axiom.** What does the principle rest on? An axiom is the rung that stops the ladder. Here
   it is a claim about how people learn and about the duty to specify work clearly. The
   business axiom for that claim is A4 in `business-first-principles`.

Two errors are common, and both are visible from the ladder.

- **The skipped rung.** A person moves from one incident to an axiom, or from an observation
  straight to a principle. The conclusion feels strong because the top of the ladder is strong.
  The middle is missing, so nothing carries the load.
- **The ground floor.** A person collects observations and refuses to climb. The work produces a
  dashboard rather than a decision.

Two tests apply at every rung. First, can you state the evidence in one sentence? Second, can
you name the result that will move you down a rung?

Worked examples of the full climb, one business and one personal, are in
`references/method.md`.

## The tests

Six tests appear in the text. They are cheap to run, and they separate a position from an
opinion.

- **Acts 17:11.** "These were more noble than those in Thessalonica, in that they received the
  word with all readiness of mind, and searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were
  so." The test: check the claim against the source, daily, with willingness to find it false.
- **1 Thessalonians 5:21.** "Prove all things; hold fast that which is good."
- **Lexical:** "Prove" is among the renderings of G1381 _dokimazo_, defined as "to test
  (literally or figuratively); by implication, to approve". The word covers testing and
  approving together. A test that can never approve anything is not this word.
- **1 John 4:1.** "Beloved, believe not every spirit, but try the spirits whether they are of
  God: because many false prophets are gone out into the world." The verse addresses claims of
  inspiration first. Applying it to persuasive claims of any kind is an inference.
- **Job 34:3.** "For the ear trieth words, as the mouth tasteth meat." Note that "meat" in 1611
  means food of any kind. The image is tasting rather than reading. **(Lexical)**
- **Proverbs 18:13.** "He that answereth a matter before he heareth it, it is folly and shame
  unto him." The test: no verdict before the whole case is heard.
- **Proverbs 18:17** is the charter for adversarial review. "He that is first in his own cause
  seemeth just; but his neighbour cometh and searcheth him." The neighbour is the part of the
  case that the first speaker did not present. A review that reads only the author's summary
  has not begun.

The practical rule from these texts is an inference. A claim is tested by someone who does not
profit from it being true, and the test runs before the decision rather than after it.
**(Inference)**

## The mind as the instrument under repair

Reasoning is done by a person. A damaged instrument produces damaged output, and no method
repairs the instrument by itself.

Scripture treats the condition of the mind as part of the work rather than as a preliminary to
it.

- **Philippians 2:5.** "Let this mind be in you, which was also in Christ Jesus." The pattern of
  thought is given rather than self-generated. **(Text states)**
- **1 Corinthians 2:16.** "For who hath known the mind of the Lord, that he may instruct him?
  But we have the mind of Christ." Note the order. The verse denies that the human mind can
  instruct God, then claims a mind that is received. **(Text states)**
- **Romans 12:1-2.** "And be not conformed to this world: but be ye transformed by the renewing
  of your mind, that ye may prove what is that good, and acceptable, and perfect, will of God."
- **Lexical:** "renewing" is G342 _anakainosis_, "renovation". "Prove" is G1381 _dokimazo_
  again, so the testing faculty itself is the thing under repair.
- **2 Corinthians 10:3-5.** The weapons are not carnal, and the work is "Casting down
  imaginations, and every high thing that exalteth itself against the knowledge of God, and
  bringing into captivity every thought to the obedience of Christ". **(Text states)** Note
  that the text names imaginations and high things rather than opinions.
- **Isaiah 26:3.** "Thou wilt keep him in perfect peace, whose mind is stayed on thee: because
  he trusteth in thee."
- **Lexical:** "stayed" is among the renderings of H5564 _samak_, "to prop (literally or
  figuratively); reflexively, to lean upon or take hold of". The word is about a support rather
  than about a feeling.
- **Colossians 3:1-2.** "If ye then be risen with Christ, seek those things which are above,
  where Christ sitteth on the right hand of God. Set your affection on things above, not on
  things on the earth." Attention is directed, and the direction is commanded. **(Text
  states)**
- **Philippians 4:8.** "Whatsoever things are true, whatsoever things are honest, whatsoever
  things are just, whatsoever things are pure, whatsoever things are lovely, whatsoever things
  are of good report; if there be any virtue, and if there be any praise, think on these
  things."
- **Lexical:** "think on" is G3049 _logizomai_, defined as "to take an inventory, i.e. estimate
  (literally or figuratively)". The command is closer to taking stock than to daydreaming.
- **Jeremiah 17:9.** "The heart is deceitful above all things, and desperately wicked: who can
  know it?" The instrument carries a bias toward its own account, and it cannot audit itself.
  **(Text states)**

The operational consequences are ordinary. **(Inference)** Sleep, fear, appetite, debt, and
loyalty all shape judgement before the reasoning starts. A frightened person will find the
conclusion that ends the fear, and the argument gets constructed afterwards. The method cannot
repair this, so the condition of the thinker belongs in the method as a stated input.

## When not to do this work

First-principles reasoning is expensive. It costs time, it unsettles settled things, and it
produces arguments that other people cannot follow quickly.

Run it when at least one of these holds:

- **The decision is hard to reverse.** A one-way door is worth the cost of a full derivation.
  The business axiom for that case is A32.
- **A pattern has failed.** The known answer was applied and the result was wrong, so the
  pattern is now the suspect.
- **Agreement arrived too quickly.** Unanimity without argument is a signal that nobody has
  examined the premise.
- **The question is about purpose.** "What is this for?" cannot be answered by a method built to
  hit a target that nobody questioned.

For everything else, apply the known pattern. A person who re-derives invoicing or password
policy is not being rigorous. They are avoiding the work. The known pattern carries the
accumulated cost of many failures, and that is an asset.

Two costs of misuse deserve names.

- **Analysis as avoidance.** The derivation continues because deciding is uncomfortable. The
  tell is a question that keeps growing without narrowing.
- **Tearing out a load-bearing wall.** A principle that is actually load-bearing gets removed
  in order to inspect it, and the structure falls during the inspection. Test a foundation with
  load rather than with demolition. **(Inference)**

## Output format

Use this shape for a first-principles answer.

1. **The question**, restated in one sentence.
2. **What is given.** The facts, constraints, and resources that were not chosen. Mark the
   uncertain ones.
3. **The load-bearing claim.** The principle or axiom the answer rests on, named, with its
   status: Text states, Lexical, Inference, or Contested.
4. **The derivation.** The steps from the given to the conclusion, one claim per line. A gap
   here is the whole point of the exercise.
5. **The tests.** What the claim was checked against, and by whom.
6. **The falsifier.** The observation that ends the argument.
7. **The limit.** What cannot be settled here, and why.
8. **Confidence.** Plainly stated versus inferred, and the cost of being wrong.

Keep the answer short when the question is small. The format is a tool for hard questions, and
not a ritual for easy ones.
